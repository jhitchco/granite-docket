Where New Hampshire says yes to housing

A 0–100 score for each town's openness to new housing, read from what it has actually permitted, the big projects moving through its boards, and the housing rules its boards are working on. Greener is more willing.

Scroll or pinch to zoom, drag to move. Click a town to see how its score is built; gray towns have no Census permit data.

The rubric

MeasurePointsHow it's scored
Building now35Housing units of every type permitted in the last three full years, per 1,000 residents (Census Building Permits Survey).
Room for apartments20Units in 5+ unit buildings permitted over the last five years plus this year, per 1,000 residents. Zero scores zero.
Big projects in the pipeline20Units in projects over 15 units named in planning and zoning records since mid-2023, per 1,000 residents. Approved or under construction count in full, proposals and concepts at half, denied or withdrawn not at all.
Picking up10Units permitted in the last three full years compared with the three before.
What boards are working on15Starts at 50. Up 10 for each housing tool named in recent records (workforce housing ordinance, inclusionary zoning, cottage courts, missing middle, smaller lots, multifamily by right, InvestNH grants, Housing Champion designation); down 20 for each limit (growth-management ordinance, permit cap, phasing ordinance, residential moratorium).

The first four measures are percentiles among the 220 towns with Census data: 100 means a town is ahead of every other town on that measure. Per-resident rates keep big cities from winning on size alone. Three-year window: 2023–2025, compared with the three years before. Bands: 65+ saying yes, 50–64 open, 35–49 middle of the pack, 20–34 cautious, under 20 little being built. Because every measure is relative, the statewide average sits near 40.

What this doesn't capture: water and sewer capacity, land prices and demand. Small rural towns can score low without anyone saying no, and a high score in a growing suburb can sit alongside heated local debate. Policy signals come from keywords in meeting records and are rougher than the permit numbers; towns whose sites can't be crawled get a neutral 50 on that measure.
